Why Sewage Cleanup Is Not a DIY Job

No homeowner should try to clean up sewage personally. Because raw sewage is a biological hazard, the work demands trained people, protective equipment (gloves, boots, respirators and Tyvek suits) and disinfection products registered with the EPA and not sold to the public. People exposed to the pathogens in sewage can become seriously ill with skin and respiratory infections, hepatitis or gastrointestinal disease.

Contaminated materials count as biohazard waste, and disposing of them properly is part of a professional cleanup. It is illegal to throw sewage-contaminated insulation, drywall or carpet in the regular trash, and doing so puts the community and the workers who collect it at risk. Licensed biohazard disposal facilities take this waste from certified restoration firms, which keep the required protocols in place.

How to Tell You Need Sewage Cleanup

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you time, money, and prevent significant damage to your property. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Sewage or dark water backing up through floor drains, toilets or tubs
  • Family members falling ill, especially with gastrointestinal trouble, for no obvious reason
  • Lower levels or drain openings giving off a heavy sewage odor, sometimes like rotten eggs
  • Gurgling sounds from toilets or drains when other fixtures are used
  • Carpet or flooring contaminated when a toilet overflowed with solid waste
  • Dark residue from sewage left on the basement floor or ringing a drain
  • Foul-smelling moisture showing up on flooring or along the walls

Maintenance Tips for Sewage Cleanup

A little prevention goes a long way in avoiding expensive restoration emergencies. Follow these tips from our IICRC-certified technicians:

  • Flush nothing but toilet paper, because wipes, even 'flushable' ones, cause clogs
  • A backwater valve at the sewer connection stops the city system from backing up into your home
  • Have roots removed where trees grow by the sewer, before they get inside the pipe and plug it
  • Stormwater from a sump or downspout should never drain into the sewer, since the extra volume invites a backup
  • Have your sewer line inspected with a camera every 2 to 3 years to catch potential problems
  • Keep cooking oil, grease and food scraps out of the drain, where they build into clogs
